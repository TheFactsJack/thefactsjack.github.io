The 2026 Midterms Were a Rebuke of Trump — and Not Just Because His Voters Stayed Home

November 9, 2026 · Based on vote counts and voter surveys. Counts are not yet certified; we’ll update the figures when they are.

The short answer

Yes, it was a rebuke of Trump. Republicans lost about twice as much ground as a president’s party usually does in a midterm, voters said Trump was a big reason, and many of his own 2024 voters switched sides.

Trump says Republicans lost because he wasn’t on the ballot and his voters stayed home. Some did, but that explains only about half of it. Even if exactly the same people had voted as in 2024, Democrats would still have won the national House vote by about 6 points.

It was a vote against Trump and the Republicans more than a vote for Democrats. On ballot measures, voters still backed several conservative positions.

How solid is this? The size of the swing comes from the vote count. What voters thought of Trump and why they voted comes from two independent voter surveys, NBC’s exit poll and the SSRS poll used by AP, Fox and CNN, which agree within a few points. The one softer number is how much came from switchers versus people staying home, because it depends on voters remembering their 2024 vote. We’ll update it when studies of actual voter records come out, usually a few months after an election.

1. How big was it?

The president’s party almost always loses ground in a midterm. Since 1950 the average loss is about 7 points on the national House vote margin. This year Republicans lost about 15. Only 2010, Obama’s first midterm, was bigger.

2. Why did voters turn away?

Voters pointed at Trump himself.

  • Low approval. Only 37–38% of voters approved of the job Trump is doing, down from 44–45% at his first midterm in 2018.
  • They said so. 44% said one reason for their vote was to oppose Trump. Only 16% said it was to support him. In 2018 it was 38% against, 26% for.
  • Prices, tariffs and the war. About 6 in 10 blamed Trump’s policies for higher prices, about two-thirds said his tariffs hurt the economy, and about 6 in 10 said the Iran war was the wrong decision.
  • Checks on the president. About 7 in 10 said Congress should do more to check him.

Sources: NBC News Exit Poll; AP Voter Poll; Fox News Voter Poll.

3. Did Trump’s voters stay home, or change sides?

Both, in roughly equal measure.

  • Some stayed home. In 2024, Trump beat Harris 49.8% to 48.3%. But among the people who voted this year, 44% said they had voted for Trump in 2024 and 49% for Harris. So this year’s voters leaned toward people who had backed Harris, which helped Democrats.
  • Some switched. About 7 in every 100 Trump voters who turned out voted for a Democrat for the House. In Texas, Ohio, Iowa, Kansas and South Carolina it was about 1 in 10.

4. Was it just local races and candidates?

No. Every kind of place moved toward Democrats by at least 14 points: Latino-majority counties (+28), cities (+18), mid-size metros (+16), big suburbs (+16) and rural areas (+14). In more than 1,500 rural counties, Republicans ran 8 points behind Trump’s 2024 share.

Candidates mattered at the edges. Florida held for Republicans, and Senate candidates Trump personally picked in Texas, North Carolina and South Carolina lost.

Sources: Michael Levy’s county tracker for the 14 Senate races (early counts); Reuters/USA Today rural county totals.

5. Did voters turn against conservative ideas?

Not across the board. On the same ballots that elected Democrats:

  • Five states banned noncitizen voting, each with more than 60%.
  • Photo ID passed in Nevada, Ohio and North Carolina (and failed in Colorado and California).
  • Limits on transgender athletes passed in Arizona, Nebraska and Washington.
  • Abortion-rights measures passed in Virginia and Nevada, and a Republican-drawn congressional map in Missouri failed by 24 points.

As one political scientist told the Christian Science Monitor, voters didn’t move sharply left on policy; they lost faith in Republicans to deliver. That cuts against anyone claiming 2026 was a mandate for a Democratic agenda.

Source: Christian Science Monitor, Nov 5–6 (some counts incomplete). No measure asked directly about tariffs or the war.

The Republican comparison was added Nov 6, after we’d seen some survey numbers, and we picked the two groups before opening any district results. We didn’t drop any measure after seeing the data.
